# Stable directional shadows in a moving celestial frame

FLIP’s cast shadow needs a sampling grid fixed to its lunar site. The camera determines which part of that grid a cascade covers. BigSpace determines which coordinates reach the GPU. The physical Sun determines the projection direction. Each can change independently. A change in coordinate representation alone should leave shadow visibility at a physical point invariant.

The baseline uses LunCoSim `3474af2f1`, BigSpace `581295f9` and Bevy `8671dfe7`. The FLIP measurements use the windowed release executable with the High preset. The first receiver-frame comparison used Bevy `6fee2ce9`. The current implementation shares a physical grid datum across views, reserves room for the selected filter and receiver bias, and identifies the scene Sun after per-view light packing. [Published Bevy revision](https://github.com/LunCoSim/bevy/commit/daed5647147acc49df25d827aa7b1d4fb7e2e122) · [Runtime contract](../architecture/render-decoupling.md#directional-shadow-reference-frames)


## Filter cost

A native Vulkan timestamp test ran the production comparison-filter functions on an RTX 5060 Laptop GPU over 1600 × 1000 pixels with a 4096-pixel depth texture. Median filter-pass times were 0.0933 ms for Gaussian and 0.0538 ms for the phase-correct 3×3 tent. Four bilinear comparisons reconstruct the tent, including its fractional texel weights. A CPU check against direct convolution over 10,000 random silhouettes had a maximum absolute difference below 3.4 × 10⁻¹⁶. These timings cover the sampling pass alone. [Separable comparison filtering](https://www.ludicon.com/castano/blog/articles/shadow-mapping-summary-part-1/)

An independent Vulkan timestamp pass exercised the shipping globe height lookup and skyline functions. It rendered a 900-pixel-diameter globe into a 1600 × 1000 target, with a repeatable 2048 × 1024 heightfield, 20 km of relief and a Sun near quadrature. Compared with `8aa772d8c`, the median Earth-scale pass fell from 0.433 to 0.122 ms and the Moon-scale pass from 0.470 to 0.191 ms. All 1.6 million 8-bit visibility values agreed at each radius, including 3,648 partially lit pixels at Earth scale and 3,878 at Moon scale. The benchmark isolates globe visibility on synthetic terrain. The orbital captures below use the actual Twin.

## What actually moved

The controlled pair in `flip_cad_drive.usda` holds the physics pose and viewing pose fixed on the surface, seeks celestial time forward by 2.5 seconds, then returns to the original epoch. The first cascade spans 49 m across 4,096 texels, or 11.963 mm per texel **in the plane perpendicular to the Sun**. The two captures give the following measurements.

|---|---:|---|

| Same light axis measured in render coordinates | 0.0000336° | Render axes and local surface axes have different relationships across the two epochs |
| Change in rendered camera translation | (329.756, 9.649, 922.462) m, magnitude 979.677 m | A fixed physical camera acquired a different representation |

| Ground-shadow crop, pixels changing by more than 10 levels in any RGB channel | 130 with the native shadow enabled, 0 disabled, 130 restored | The native directional map accounts for the conspicuous changes in this crop |

Returning to the first epoch reproduced its scene pixels exactly. In the separate live run the parked chassis was asleep. A verified drive moved the chassis 2.75 m and also contained a 1.45 km change in render coordinates. The driving captures include genuine motion of the vehicle and shadow. They establish that the two effects coexist. Quantifying the artificial component while driving needs a point tracked on the ground or rover across images.

The angle above uses the normalized light axis projected into the same rigid part’s normalized basis at each epoch. The translation-only calculation projects the render displacement onto each of the **first** light’s X/Y axes, divides by the native texel pitch, and takes the signed remainder modulo one texel. The measured phases use the native clip matrices and the same caster origin. The small residual after camera subtraction uses a physically stationary camera as a measuring reference. A grid tied to a driving camera would slide along the terrain. These results also do not identify the exact sequence of BigSpace cell crossings between captures. Raw audits, images and the two-frame edge crop are in the investigation checkout under `target/shadow-investigation/`.

At a grazing Sun angle a long shadow can show genuine motion from a tiny change in direction. On level ground a vertical caster of height *h* has shadow length *h cot(e)* at elevation *e*. For illustration, at 5° a 1 m caster and a 6.14 µrad change entirely in elevation move the tip about **0.81 mm**. That is an example, not a measurement of FLIP’s local terrain slope or Sun elevation. Grazing light also stretches the ground footprint of a shadow texel in the down-Sun direction. A depth sample switching at a thin panel or wheel can brighten or darken several screen pixels. The reported 0.460 texel is a sampling displacement in the light plane, not a measurement of the physical shadow tip.

## Where the instability enters

1. **BigSpace serves rendering precision.** LunCoSim owns one persistent `OriginAnchor`. The viewport resolves the selected camera into the canonical `WorldGrid` and writes a cell and remainder. The pinned BigSpace owner seeds the floating origin’s *parent grid* with the origin **cell center**, zero local remainder and identity rotation, then propagates the origin through the nested grids. A body-fixed site can therefore acquire different render translations and rotations as its celestial ancestors advance. Rasterizing nearby geometry in those coordinates is valid. The coordinate system that makes vertices precise does not by itself provide a permanent origin for a shadow pattern. [Viewport origin reconciliation](../../crates/lunco-usd-bevy-camera/src/camera_switch.rs) · [Pinned BigSpace origin propagation](https://github.com/LunCoSim/big_space/blob/581295f909ddddad138a876b497dbc1fdff389b6/src/grid/local_origin.rs#L464-L495)
2. **Bevy already stabilizes cascade size.** It derives the diameter from the slice diagonals in camera space, then fits an orthographic light view and snaps its X/Y center to integral texel pitches. Its light basis has zero translation. The anchor of its snap is consequently render-world zero. After a common translation by *T*, a fixed surface point picks up the fractional part of *dot(lightAxis,T)/pitch*. Changing to doubles for the same expression would calculate that unwanted shift more accurately. [Pinned cascade construction](https://github.com/LunCoSim/bevy/blob/8671dfe743d284f074587862e03bcd3e0b79ecc6/crates/bevy_light/src/cascade.rs#L221-L248) · [Native fitting and snapping](https://github.com/LunCoSim/bevy/blob/8671dfe743d284f074587862e03bcd3e0b79ecc6/crates/bevy_light/src/cascade.rs#L264-L345)
3. **Celestial time has two real consumers.** The simulation samples body poses at authoritative interval boundaries. Presentation updates its celestial hierarchy under an angular error budget. The scene Sun resolves its direction into the light parent, and the finalized scene-light transform supplies the render ray consumed by terrain and sky. A second ephemeris evaluation in the cascade builder could mix epochs or disagree with visible lighting. The same-frame precedent exists in LunCoSim’s terrain blueprint grid, which uses the finalized site transform after BigSpace propagation to make an authored grid stable on the ground. [Physical sampling](../../crates/lunco-celestial-spatial/src/sampling.rs) · [Presentation cadence](../../crates/lunco-celestial-spatial/src/cadence.rs) · [Terrain and Sun frame contract](../architecture/45-big-space-correct-usage.md#terrain-attached-render-coordinates)
4. **There is a separate roll hazard.** The scene Sun switches its orientation guide from local Y to local X when the absolute dot product with Y exceeds 0.99. The beam direction can remain continuous while the square shadow grid rotates about it. The actual A/B capture had a small change in both the light direction and the cascade X axis relative to the chassis. It gives no evidence of a roll jump in this event. The boundary still matters for a general solution. [Scene-light orientation](../../crates/lunco-environment/src/solar.rs)

## What established renderers do

Guerrilla’s published *Killzone 2* technique holds the directional cascade footprint stable under camera rotation. Microsoft’s shadow-map guidance describes both stable extent and translating the projection in whole texels. Bevy already implements these ideas. LunCoSim’s celestial scene adds a requirement to carry the reference used for snapping through changes of render coordinates. [Guerrilla presenter notes](https://www.guerrilla-games.com/media/News/Files/GDC09_Valient_Rendering_Technology_Of_Killzone_2_Extended_Presenter_Notes.pdf) · [Microsoft guidance on shimmering](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/windows/win32/dxtecharts/common-techniques-to-improve-shadow-depth-maps#moving-the-light-in-texel-sized-increments)

Google Filament provides a direct implementation precedent. It computes a light-space reference from the world transform in double precision, projects that reference into the focused shadow map, and removes its fractional texel offset before publishing the projection. Its choice to align on even texels is specific to its implementation. Unity HDRP separately documents how camera-relative vertices, lights and matrices share a rendering coordinate conversion. These examples support retaining small GPU coordinates while carrying an explicit physical reference into the projection. The choice of a lunar site, its local axes and its lifetime is LunCoSim’s responsibility. [Filament reference construction](https://github.com/google/filament/blob/main/filament/src/details/Scene.cpp#L583-L607) · [Filament projection alignment](https://github.com/google/filament/blob/main/filament/src/ShadowMap.cpp#L766-L797) · [Unity camera-relative rendering](https://docs.unity.cn/Packages/com.unity.render-pipelines.high-definition@7.3/manual/Camera-Relative-Rendering.html)

## Recommended design

**Specify the invariant at the physical receiver.** At a fixed epoch, a fixed surface point illuminated by a fixed Sun must retain its fractional shadow coordinates when its render representation changes. When the camera translates, the covered texels may move by whole cells. When the Sun, caster or receiver actually moves, the shadow must move with the geometry.

For a cascade with texel pitch *s*, let *A* be a persistent nearby point fixed in the receiving surface’s frame, *u* and *v* be the light-plane unit axes in that frame, *C* be the unsnapped center of the fitted cascade, and *P* a receiver. In one axis the rule is

```text
centerIndex = floor(dot(u, C − A) / s)
sampleX     = dot(u, P − A) / s − centerIndex + mapWidth / 2
```

The Y axis follows the same construction with the native texture orientation. Coordinate translations cancel in the differences. Re-expressing both the vectors and axes under the same rigid rotation preserves their dot products. The camera can change the integer center without moving the fractional grid beneath a stationary point. This is a CPU construction of the projection. The shader still receives the ordinary native matrix.

**Reuse the spatial owner for the reference.** For FLIP and Summer Space School that reference is the nearby authored site origin on the body-fixed surface. Bring its origin and orientation into the same finalized render frame as the geometry. Use existing cell-aware, f64 pose and vector operations for semantic inputs and reference arithmetic. Compose in double or in bounded relative coordinates, then narrow at the native GPU matrix boundary. Independently reconstructing an astronomical absolute position and subtracting the camera after conversion to f32 discards the precision this design requires. Also bound error from the final f32 matrices and vertex coordinates. [Site placement](../../crates/lunco-celestial-spatial/src/placement.rs) · [Shared coordinate conversions](../../crates/lunco-spatial/src/coords.rs)

**Choose a datum that matches the view’s subject and scale.** A main view and an instrument observing the same site use the same site reference and their own camera frusta, splits and texel pitches. A studio preview has its own studio frame. An inertial observation may need an inertial scene datum, while an orbital camera looking down at a lunar site can still use a body-fixed receiving frame. Bind this choice to the exact output, observed domain and scene generation. A global physics-frame lookup or the first active camera cannot distinguish simultaneous views. Retire the binding when its scene or view is retired. [Per-output view identity](../../crates/lunco-render/src/camera.rs) · [Celestial reference frames](../../crates/lunco-celestial/src/registry.rs)

**Bound the distance to the datum for long traverses.** A real rotation of the light basis by a small angle *δθ* moves a point’s light-plane coordinate by up to roughly *distance(P,A) × δθ*. At lunar radius, 6.14 µrad gives a lever arm of about **10.7 m**. At 20 m it gives about **0.12 mm**. Using the Moon’s center everywhere would satisfy translation invariance and still yield poor near-surface results. For a regional traverse, select a nearby body-fixed surface patch when coverage requires it. At a handoff with the same basis and pitch, carry the prior fractional phase, including the projected vector between the old and new datums modulo each cascade’s pitch. A patch boundary or BigSpace cell boundary must never silently reset the phase. A change of tangent axes also requires basis continuity. Record an explicit replay/seek rule for chart transitions before enabling this for continuous planetary navigation. A fixed authored site needs no evolving phase history.

**Give the rasterizer ownership of its two transverse axes.** A circular solar emitter supplies a ray and angular extent. OpenUSD represents a distant light by emission along local −Z. A Bevy light adapter therefore needs a rotation, but many rotations aim −Z in the same direction. The present Y/X switch avoids a near-zero cross product when constructing that rotation. Native cascade fitting inherits the whole rotation, including the arbitrary choice of transverse axes. A synthetic calculation used the representable f32 Y values immediately below and above the 0.99 threshold, with equal positive X and Z components chosen for unit rays. The directions differ by about 0.000049°. Their transverse axes differ by about 134.7°, or 44.7° even after accounting for quarter-turn symmetry of a square lattice. This is a calculation from the current branch rule. The measured FLIP event occurred away from this boundary. [OpenUSD distant-light convention](https://openusd.org/release/api/class_usd_lux_distant_light.html) · [Scene Sun adapter](../../crates/lunco-environment/src/solar.rs)

For Earth- and Moon-surface cascades, use the receiving body's existing IAU north axis as the guide and the presented ray as the projection direction, both in the same receiver frame. Normalize their cross product for one transverse axis and construct the other by crossing back with the ray. Earth and lunar sunlight admit a well-conditioned body-pole guide even when the Sun is directly above a local site. Sweep the supported ephemeris range and assert the minimum cross-product norm. Identical epoch, frame and light then give identical axes after a seek. The nearby site still supplies the positional reference. For an authored steerable light, an authored transverse axis supplies the missing information. A direction alone cannot define a globally continuous tangent basis over the whole sphere. General light tracks reaching a guide's pole require an authored orientation or a deterministic continuous frame along the track with reproducible evaluation at seeks. This is a local raster obligation. Globe photometry, analytic eclipse visibility and physical sensors have no reason to inherit a square map’s roll. [Existing body-pole owner](../../crates/lunco-celestial/src/registry.rs)

**Fit and publish one consistent cascade.** Prefer passing the already-resolved reference and axes into the native fitting step before the X/Y snap. The generic renderer needs a reference in its own coordinates and has no need to know about celestial time, USD or the Moon. Preserve native frustum coverage, reverse-Z fitting, cascade overlap, bias units, filter footprint and texel pitch. Use the same finalized projection for caster frusta, candidate culling, receiver scissor, depth rendering, forward lookup and terrain’s displaced geometry. The camera driving terrain LOD remains the actual view. The reference only controls the shadow lattice.

There is also a public application-side seam. Bevy’s `Cascades` exposes `world_from_cascade`, `clip_from_cascade`, `clip_from_world` and `texel_size`. A translation-only adjustment can align a fitted cascade to a reference if coverage including the filter guard is proven after the shift. Run it inside `UpdateDirectionalLightCascades`, after native fitting and finalized BigSpace poses, before the set completes. Both native frustum updates and LunCoSim’s receiver crop depend on that boundary. Update the complete transform consistently. An arbitrary post-fit roll also changes which frustum corners fit, so it belongs in fitting. If the public seam cannot guarantee coverage and roll, the clean solution is a small opt-in input to the native fitter. It avoids implementing a second cascade builder in LunCoSim. A dependency change would require separate authorization. [Native light schedule](https://github.com/LunCoSim/bevy/blob/8671dfe743d284f074587862e03bcd3e0b79ecc6/crates/bevy_light/src/lib.rs#L186-L242) · [Native raster and sampling inputs](https://github.com/LunCoSim/bevy/blob/8671dfe743d284f074587862e03bcd3e0b79ecc6/crates/bevy_pbr/src/render/light.rs#L1949-L2022) · [LunCoSim receiver bounds](../../crates/lunco-render-bevy/src/shadow_receiver_crop.rs)

**Keep celestial cadence and terrain occlusion independent.** Quantizing the Sun angle or updating the light less frequently would accumulate a real change and release it as a larger step. Re-centering BigSpace on one rover would couple every other camera to that rover. A larger atlas or wider filter can reduce the apparent edge error at a resource cost while leaving the phase dependent on the floating origin. The terrain horizon cache has its own angular validity rule, and physical solar visibility has its own terrain-query owner. The FLIP on/off experiment identifies native directional shadow maps as the source of the high-contrast changes in the tested crop. It supplies no reason to retune those other systems. ## From a rover wheel to the Earth–Moon system

The shared physical question is which directions on the apparent solar disc reach a particular receiver at a specified epoch. The source can be an ephemeris body with position, distance and radius, or an authored distant light with direction and angular diameter. Preserve that distinction at the source. A receiver on Earth and one on the Moon can consequently have different apparent directions, angular sizes and eclipse conditions at the same epoch. Use the existing ephemeris, frame and irradiance owners. The renderer consumes coherent presentation samples; instruments and power models use their declared sampling contracts at the physical tick. This design does not silently redefine the existing panel port, which measures a fraction of spatial samples with clear Sun-centre rays. [Current instrument contract](../architecture/terrain-layered-rendering.md#shadow-ownership)

| Effect | Current path | Recommended representation |
|---|---|---|
| A rover, lander, habitat or local relief casts onto another surface | Native directional cascades cover nearby mesh interactions. | Rasterize actual caster geometry into the reference-stable cascades above. Bound the work by each view’s useful footprint. A grazing wheel edge and the underside of a chassis need this geometric detail. |
| A ridge hides the Sun over a site or large part of a globe | Site terrain has a direction-dependent visibility cache. A displaced globe fragment estimates the skyline from up to 64 curved heightfield samples and integrates the apparent solar disc at that skyline. Globe shells explicitly disable native shadow casting and reception. | Bake a body-fixed directional horizon from composed elevation, including curvature and terrain beyond the visible tile. At render time, evaluate the current solar disc against the skyline. Share the derived terrain data across site and orbital views. Compressed angular coefficients are a published candidate; select the representation from measured angular error at narrow peaks, bandwidth and bake cost. |
| Day/night on a smooth globe, or another body crossing the Sun | Globe materials already shade the surface normal against the selected light. The celestial `Occultation` query tests a segment against body spheres and reports a Boolean. | Compute local incidence with the body surface normal and evaluate the visible solar disc near the geometric limb. Compute external eclipses from the apparent foreground silhouettes at the receiver. Use analytic disc overlap for a supported spherical approximation; ellipsoidal limbs or an atmospheric transmittance model can refine the cases that resolve those effects. |

[Globe shell intent](../../crates/lunco-celestial-spatial/src/globe_lod.rs) · [Current globe heightfield visibility](../../assets/shaders/blueprint.wesl) · [Current occultation primitive](../../crates/lunco-celestial-spatial/src/queries.rs) · [Existing LunCoSim horizon-map design](../../crates/lunco-environment/FOURIER_HORIZON_MAPS.md) · [Fritsch et al., planetary terrain shadows](https://graphics.tu-bs.de/upload/publications/fritsch2025fchorizonmaps/fritsch2025fchorizonmaps.pdf)

**Compose by occluder and coverage.** A rover being observed from orbit still casts a shadow when its projected footprint is significant. A mountain outside the camera frustum can shadow that rover. And an eclipse affects the rover, its solar panels and the visible terrain at once. Altitude alone cannot choose the algorithm. Use projected footprint to select detail and use a single owner for each blocker over a stated coverage region. Where two terrain levels represent the same ridge, blend estimates of the same visibility through a registered overlap, preserving a shared skyline across tile seams. A horizon baked at ground height needs a validity range for elevated receivers. For a ridge at range *d*, raising the receiver by *h* can change its apparent elevation by order *h/d* radians. When that exceeds the admitted angular error, evaluate visibility at the actual receiver height or refine against the terrain hierarchy. A heightfield cannot describe an overhang or a spacecraft.

**Compose partial shadows before averaging.** For each incident ray the test is whether *any* opaque blocker intercepts it. For a finite disc, integrate the unblocked directions with incidence and material response. Two blockers covering the same half of the Sun leave half the disc visible; two covering complementary halves leave none. Multiplying two 0.5 visibility values would yield 0.25 in both scenes. A filtered native shadow-map value likewise describes a spatial filter and does not identify which half of the solar disc is covered. Use cheap fully lit/blocked bounds and a single-owner estimate for ordinary pixels. For a resolved overlap of partial silhouettes, retain angular information or evaluate a bounded set of common disc directions and intersect visibility ray by ray. Doing this exactly for arbitrary mesh penumbrae would require extra information beyond the current depth-map filter. Treat that as a separately benchmarked fidelity feature. Apply visibility to the selected Sun's direct light once; diffuse fill, Earthshine, emissive materials and atmospheric scattering have their own contributions. At the smooth terminator, integrate the positive incident directions, so a visible sliver of Sun can contribute while its centre is below the horizon. Airless lunar twilight and atmospheric terrestrial twilight are different phenomena. [Existing material response](../../assets/shaders/terrain_surface.wesl) · [Schneegans et al., physically based real-time eclipses](https://elib.dlr.de/215927/1/Computer%20Graphics%20Forum%20-%202025%20-%20Schneegans%20-%20Physically%20Based%20Real%E2%80%90Time%20Rendering%20of%20Eclipses.pdf)

**Budget the expensive work where its error is visible.** Stable local projections reuse the atlas. Static terrain horizon assets can be generated asynchronously and reused through a complete solar day; fence publication by body, datum, tile coverage and terrain revision. A terrain edit must also invalidate affected receiver horizons downrange, including neighboring tiles. Compare the proposed precomputed horizon to the existing angular cache and globe march before replacing either. For an eclipse, use a conservative angular or shadow-cone bound for each receiving body or tile. Fully clear and fully obscured tiles have constant external visibility. Evaluate apparent overlap only in the candidate footprint, in receiver-relative coordinates derived from f64 celestial geometry. Test the foreground ordering and self-body horizon explicitly. Use consistent apparent/geometric positions and time conventions when comparing ingress/egress with an independent ephemeris. NASA SPICE provides a reference for none, partial, annular and total occultations; those classifications alone do not give a fractional irradiance. [NASA NAIF occultation reference](https://naif.jpl.nasa.gov/pub/naif/toolkit_docs/C/cspice/occult_c.html)

The intended cost is bounded arithmetic per active directional-light/view/cascade and shared frame resolution per view. There is no need for additional depth maps, samples per pixel, GPU passes, per-caster traversal or more frequent ephemeris updates. Raster work can still change if a different bound admits more draws, which is why both GPU and frame timings are part of acceptance. Filtering can be assessed separately for residual aliasing of genuinely moving thin geometry after the coordinate defect is fixed.
